\title[idmapaudit: auditing gene-ID-mapping sensitivity]{idmapaudit: an audit framework for gene-identifier-mapping sensitivity in pathway enrichment analysis}

\author[1,$\ast$]{Ethan Y.\ Ye}

\address[1]{\orgdiv{}, \orgname{Independent Researcher}, \orgaddress{\country{}}}

\corresp[$\ast$]{Corresponding author. \href{mailto:ethan.ye.201010@gmail.com}{ethan.ye.201010@gmail.com}}

\abstract{
\textbf{Motivation:} Pathway enrichment pipelines convert a differential expression result into a different gene-identifier namespace before testing it against a pathway database, and this conversion step is rarely audited despite being lossy: identifiers fail to map, map many-to-one or one-to-many, and drift out of currency relative to the databases being queried. Existing identifier-mapping frameworks (BridgeDb, g:Profiler/g:Convert) make conversion easier but do not treat the choice of namespace and resolution policy as an experimental factor with a measurable, per-pathway effect on enrichment calls.\\
\textbf{Results:} We present \texttt{idmapaudit}, an R package and \texttt{targets} pipeline that fixes a differential expression result and varies only the gene-ID-mapping strategy used to build the enrichment query list and background, crossing the Ensembl/Entrez/Symbol namespace choice with the one-to-many resolution policy (drop, first-hit, keep-all). For every pathway, the tool reports a fragility score and a binary-entropy stability index that score ``always significant'' and ``never significant'' calls as equally stable and mid-range calls as fragile, together with a gene-attrition regression and a mapping-noise null model that isolates mapping-structure-driven instability from finite-list-size sampling effects. The stability-metric core has no Bioconductor dependency and passes 35 unit tests (125 expectations) against hand-computed values, including regression tests for three implementation bugs and three pipeline-crashing defects identified and fixed during development. An accompanying base-R case study (not using \texttt{idmapaudit} itself) on two independently generated public processings of the airway dexamethasone dataset (GSE52778) quantifies a background-list divergence that shrinks from 69\% to 3.7\% once an expression filter is applied (an instance of the single most common ORA background-choice error reported in the literature), and a marker-panel-based sample-identity recovery procedure that caps at 75\% accuracy even under calibration, a quantified limit on relabeling public data by expression alone. Full pathway-level fragility scoring against GO, KEGG, Reactome, and WikiPathways is implemented and unit-tested; its continuous-integration job installs the full annotation stack without error, but the pipeline has not yet completed an end-to-end run against real data, and no pathway-level fragility scores on real data are reported here.\\
\textbf{Availability and implementation:} \texttt{idmapaudit} is implemented in R and available under the MIT license at \url{https://github.com/ethanyyy123/bioadvances}, together with the \texttt{targets} pipeline, the full methods note, and the real-data case-study scripts.\\

\textbf{Supplementary information:} The per-pathway fragility lookup table format, the synthetic worked example, and the unit-test suite are included in the linked repository.}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

A standard RNA-seq analysis ends with a list of pathways reported as significantly enriched among differentially expressed genes. Between the differential expression (DE) step and that list sits a conversion that is rarely discussed in Methods sections: the DE result's gene identifiers, typically Ensembl gene IDs emitted by the aligner or quantifier, must be translated into whatever namespace the chosen pathway database indexes on. That translation is lossy. Genes fail to map; genes map to more than one identifier in the target namespace; and the resolution policy applied to that ambiguity -- drop the gene, keep an arbitrary first hit, or keep every hit -- is rarely stated, let alone justified or varied to test its effect on the result.

The scale of the problem is established. \citet{wadi2016outdated} showed that stale Gene Ontology annotations measurably change enrichment calls; \citet{tomczak2018gene} showed the same for GO's evolution over time more broadly; and \citet{wijesooriya2022orabackground} surveyed published over-representation analyses (ORA) and found that roughly 95\% used an inappropriate background gene list, a closely related input-sensitivity failure this paper's case study independently reproduces (Section~\ref{sec:real-universe}). Two frameworks address the mapping side of the problem directly: BridgeDb \citep{vaniersel2010bridgedb} provides standardized identifier-mapping services, and g:Profiler's g:Convert \citep{kolberg2023gprofiler} couples identifier conversion with enrichment and supports custom, versioned backgrounds. Neither treats the mapping strategy itself as an experimental factor whose effect on a specific enrichment result is measured and reported per pathway. The closest work in spirit is \citet{xie2023dexbenchmark}'s Dex-Benchmark, which benchmarks differential-expression \emph{algorithm} choice (DESeq2 versus limma versus other methods) against dexamethasone perturbation data and ChIP-seq-derived glucocorticoid-receptor (GR/NR3C1) target gene sets, holding the identifier space fixed throughout -- the inverse of the factor this paper varies.

We present \texttt{idmapaudit}, which fixes the DE algorithm and result and varies only the identifier-mapping strategy, producing a per-pathway \emph{fragility score}: the fraction of mapping branches under which a pathway's significance call is unchanged. Given existing identifier-mapping infrastructure (BridgeDb, g:Convert), we position the contribution specifically: to our knowledge this is the first per-pathway, branch-resolved quantification of mapping-choice sensitivity, rather than the first tool to touch identifier mapping or enrichment robustness individually. Concretely, this paper contributes:
\begin{unlist}
\item a factorial ID-mapping design (five namespace resolvers, two resolution policies) that isolates mapping choice as the sole manipulated variable against a fixed DE result (Section~\ref{sec:design});
\item a fragility score and binary-entropy stability index, a gene-attrition regression, and a mapping-noise null model that separates mapping-structure-driven instability from finite-sample-size effects (Sections~\ref{sec:fragility}--\ref{sec:null});
\item an open-source R implementation whose stability-metric core has no Bioconductor dependency and is unit-tested against hand-computed values, including regression tests for six bugs found during development (Section~\ref{sec:validation}); and
\item an empirical case study on two independently processed public forms of the airway dexamethasone dataset (GSE52778; \citealp{himes2014rnaseq}) that surfaces a real background-choice sensitivity and a quantified limit on recovering sample identity from expression alone (Section~\ref{sec:real}).
\end{unlist}

\section{Methods}\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Design: one fixed input, one manipulated variable}\label{sec:design}

The audit holds three things fixed and manipulates exactly one. Fixed: (i) the differential expression step, run once per dataset in the native (unversioned Ensembl) identifier space produced by the quantifier, using \texttt{DESeq2} \citep{love2014deseq2} with a standard count-threshold filter and a single named contrast; (ii) the enrichment battery -- over-representation analysis (ORA) via \texttt{clusterProfiler} \citep{yu2012clusterprofiler} and \texttt{ReactomePA} \citep{yu2016reactomepa} against GO Biological Process \citep{ashburner2000go}, KEGG \citep{kanehisa2000kegg}, Reactome \citep{fabregat2018reactome}, and WikiPathways \citep{martens2021wikipathways}, with gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA; \citealp{subramanian2005gsea}) via \texttt{fgsea} \citep{korotkevich2021fgsea} planned as a second mode; and (iii) the false-discovery-rate procedure, applied independently within every branch rather than pooled across branches. Not every database is computable against every branch, a real constraint of the underlying tools rather than a design choice (Section~\ref{sec:software}): only \texttt{enrichGO()} accepts an arbitrary key type, so GO is run across all primary and resolution-policy branches, while KEGG/Reactome/WikiPathways -- which accept only Entrez-family IDs -- are run only across the Entrez-space branches. Manipulated: the identifier-mapping branch used to build the enrichment query list and its background from the DE step's fixed output.

Running the DE step once, rather than once per branch, is a deliberate design choice: it isolates identifier-mapping as the sole manipulated variable rather than confounding it with re-computed differential-expression statistics that would themselves vary by chance across branches, ruling out by construction the alternative explanation that observed instability merely reflects DE-method disagreement.

\subsection{The ID-mapping matrix}\label{sec:matrix}

The primary factor is namespace choice, implemented as a set of \emph{resolvers} -- functions that take the union of a DE result's query and background gene lists and return every raw (possibly one-to-many) mapping edge into a target namespace:

\begin{unlist}
\item unversioned Ensembl gene ID (native; identity mapping);
\item versioned Ensembl gene ID (\texttt{ENSG00000141510.16}) -- deliberately included as a positive control, since silently failing to strip version suffixes is a common real-world bug that should produce dramatic, almost trivial instability against any pathway database keyed on the unversioned ID;
\item Entrez Gene ID via \texttt{org.Hs.eg.db}/\texttt{AnnotationDbi} \citep{huber2015bioconductor};
\item HGNC/official symbol via \texttt{org.Hs.eg.db}; and
\item HGNC/official symbol via \texttt{biomaRt} \citep{durinck2009biomart} -- a second resolver for nominally the same namespace as (iv), included because the two can legitimately disagree, which is itself a reportable finding rather than a bug to reconcile.
\end{unlist}

A secondary factor, the one-to-many/many-to-one \emph{resolution policy}, is applied only to the Entrez and org.Hs.eg.db-symbol resolvers, where ambiguity is common: \textbf{drop} removes any native ID with more than one distinct target; \textbf{first} keeps the first-seen target per native ID; \textbf{list} keeps every edge, exploding one-to-many keys into duplicate rows. This is deliberately kept as a small sub-experiment restricted to two resolvers rather than folded into a full second factorial, since the full cross of namespaces $\times$ policies $\times$ databases $\times$ modes produces more cells than can be interpreted cleanly.

For every resulting branch, the query list and the background list are mapped \emph{independently}, and their attrition rates are reported separately (Eq.~\ref{eq:attrition}). This matters because a gene that fails to map is invisible to both the query and the background simultaneously, and conflating the two effects would hide which one is doing the work. Attrition is defined on native keys that found at least one target, not on the size of the mapped image: an image-size ratio is the wrong quantity whenever the mapping is not one-to-one, since many-to-one collapse shrinks the image without losing any gene, and one-to-many expansion under the \textbf{list} policy can grow it past $|G|$ entirely:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Attrition}_b(G) = 1 - \frac{|\,\{g \in G : g \in \mathrm{keys}(\mathrm{map}_b)\}\,|}{|G|},
\label{eq:attrition}
\end{equation}
i.e.\ one minus the fraction of native IDs in $G$ that appear as a `from`-key of branch $b$'s resolved mapping table at all, regardless of how many targets each maps to, computed once for the query list $G = G_{\mathrm{sig}}$ and once for the background.

\subsection{The audit / fragility score}\label{sec:fragility}

For a pathway $p$ tested in $k_p$ of the $k$ mapping branches (branches where $p$ has zero mapped genes are excluded from the denominator entirely, not counted as "not significant"), let $\mathrm{sig}_{p,b}\in\{0,1\}$ indicate whether $p$ is called significant at FDR $<\alpha$ in branch $b$. The fragility proportion is
\begin{equation}
F_p = \frac{1}{k_p}\sum_{b} \mathrm{sig}_{p,b} \in [0,1].
\label{eq:fragility}
\end{equation}
$F_p$ near 0 or near 1 both indicate stability (stably absent, stably present); values in between indicate a call that depends on the mapping branch. To make "how stable" a single, symmetric number, we report the binary entropy
\begin{equation}
H_p = -F_p\log_2 F_p - (1-F_p)\log_2(1-F_p),
\label{eq:entropy}
\end{equation}
with the convention $0\log_2 0 := 0$, and the Bernoulli variance $V_p = F_p(1-F_p)$ as a monotone-equivalent alternative. Both are 0 at $F_p\in\{0,1\}$ and maximal at $F_p=0.5$; a pathway significant in 5 of 6 branches ($F_p=5/6$, $H_p\approx0.650$) is thereby scored as \emph{less} fragile than one significant in 3 of 6 ($F_p=0.5$, $H_p=1$), rather than treating any mid-range hit as equally unstable. Pathways are classified \textbf{stable} if $F_p\le\tau_{lo}$ or $F_p\ge\tau_{hi}$ and \textbf{fragile} otherwise, with default thresholds $\tau_{lo}=0.1$, $\tau_{hi}=0.9$. These defaults need revisiting as a function of $k$, the number of branches crossed: at the six primary branches of Section~\ref{sec:matrix}, $F_p$ only takes values in $\{0,1/6,\ldots,1\}$, and $1/6\approx0.167$ already exceeds $\tau_{lo}=0.1$ while $5/6\approx0.833$ already falls short of $\tau_{hi}=0.9$ -- so \textbf{stable} reduces to unanimous agreement, and the entropy index (Eq.~\ref{eq:entropy}) affects only the ranking of already-fragile pathways. Compounding this, the versioned-Ensembl branch is deliberately built to disagree with the rest, so it alone is often enough to push an otherwise-robust pathway out of \textbf{stable}. Thresholds should therefore either scale with $k$, or the positive-control branch should be excluded from the classification denominator and reported separately as an instrument check; neither is implemented yet, and the toy example of Section~\ref{sec:example} is constructed at $k=6$ precisely so this degeneracy is visible in Table~\ref{tab:toy} rather than hidden.

Two further pieces complete the framework and are implemented but not exercised on real data in this manuscript (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}): a gene-attrition regression that relates $H_p$ to the branch-level attrition rate (Eq.~\ref{eq:attrition}) to attribute pathway-level instability to specific mapping failures, separately for query-list and background-list attrition; and a cross-dataset agreement statistic (simple agreement rate and Fleiss' $\kappa$; \citealp{fleiss1971kappa}) for asking whether a pathway's stable/fragile classification is a property of its gene-set definition (reproducible across biologically independent datasets) or of one dataset's noise.

\subsection{Mapping-noise null}\label{sec:null}

A pathway's raw $H_p$ conflates two effects: instability caused by the specific, non-random genes that a given mapping strategy fails to translate, and instability that any finite-size random gene-list perturbation of the same magnitude would produce regardless of which genes were dropped. To isolate the first from the second, for a branch with observed attrition rate $\pi_b$ we draw $R$ replicates in which $\lfloor \pi_b n\rfloor$ genes are removed from the query list \emph{uniformly at random} (and likewise for the background), recompute the same stability statistics on each replicate, and report the observed statistic's excess over the resulting null distribution:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{excess} = x_{\mathrm{obs}} - \mathrm{median}(x_{\mathrm{null}}^{(1..R)}), \quad
z = \frac{x_{\mathrm{obs}}-\overline{x_{\mathrm{null}}}}{\mathrm{sd}(x_{\mathrm{null}})}.
\label{eq:excess}
\end{equation}
Only the excess, not the raw statistic, supports a claim that instability is driven by the \emph{structure} of real mapping failures (poorly annotated or recently renamed genes drop out disproportionately) rather than by list size alone.

\section{Software implementation}\label{sec:software}

\texttt{idmapaudit} is an R package with four modules. \texttt{R/stability-metrics.R} implements Eqs.~\ref{eq:attrition}--\ref{eq:excess} and the set-level Jaccard/overlap-coefficient and Spearman rank-concordance statistics used to compare significant-pathway sets pairwise across branches; it has no Bioconductor dependency. \texttt{R/mapping-matrix.R} implements the drop/first/list resolution policies and the per-branch query/background mapping builder described in Section~\ref{sec:matrix}. \texttt{R/enrichment-battery.R} wraps the ORA calls and generates mapping-noise-null replicates (Section~\ref{sec:null}); it declares \texttt{clusterProfiler}, \texttt{ReactomePA}, \texttt{org.Hs.eg.db}, \texttt{AnnotationDbi}, \texttt{biomaRt}, and \texttt{fgsea} as \texttt{Suggests} rather than hard dependencies, so the core metric logic can be tested and used without installing the full annotation/enrichment stack. \texttt{R/fragility-lookup.R} builds and exports the per-pathway fragility table as a flat TSV file -- the tool's intended reusable, community-facing artifact.

An accompanying \texttt{targets} pipeline \citep{landau2021targets} orchestrates the airway prototype end to end: a fixed \texttt{DESeq2} contrast on the \texttt{airway} \citep{morgan2020summarizedexperiment,himes2014rnaseq} count matrix, the five resolvers of Section~\ref{sec:matrix} with the Entrez/Symbol resolution-policy sub-branches, the ORA battery restricted per branch to the databases that branch's key type actually supports (Section~\ref{sec:design}: GO for every branch, KEGG/Reactome/WikiPathways for the two Entrez-space branches only), and the resulting fragility table. Continuous integration runs a fast pure-R unit-test job on every push and a second job that installs the full Bioconductor stack and executes this pipeline as a smoke test; both are reported in Section~\ref{sec:validation}.

\section{Validation}\label{sec:validation}

Because the stability-metric layer (Section~\ref{sec:fragility}) is pure R with no external numerical dependency, every function is unit-tested against a hand-derived, closed-form answer rather than only checked to "run without error." Table~\ref{tab:tests} reports the actual test-suite output from this manuscript's accompanying repository, run with \texttt{testthat} \citep{wickham2011testthat}. All 35 test cases (125 individual expectations) currently pass, including regression tests for six bugs found and fixed during development and review rather than left as known issues: a one-to-many mapping bug (an earlier implementation used named-vector indexing to look up mapped identifiers, which under R's \texttt{[} semantics silently keeps only the first match for a duplicated key); an attrition-rate definition that was wrong under many-to-one collapse and could go negative under one-to-many expansion (Eq.~\ref{eq:attrition}); a cross-dataset-agreement statistic that returned \texttt{NaN} rather than \texttt{NA} for a single-rater input; and three pipeline crashes (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}) surfaced only once the full \texttt{targets} pipeline executed against real data: total mapping failure -- the versioned-Ensembl positive control's designed behavior -- raising a hard error inside \texttt{clusterProfiler::enrichGO()} instead of returning an informative empty result; \texttt{clusterProfiler::enrichWP()} requiring an explicit \texttt{organism} argument that neither \texttt{enrichKEGG()} nor \texttt{enrichPathway()} require; and an unreachable live Ensembl BioMart web service (two of five resolvers depend on it) halting the entire ID-mapping step, including for resolvers with no such dependency, rather than being contained to the affected branch.

\section{Empirical case study: GSE52778}\label{sec:real}

This case study analyzes two files downloaded directly from the public GEO record for GSE52778 -- the glucocorticoid-response airway smooth-muscle series \citep{himes2014rnaseq} that motivates this project. The two files are independent, GEO-hosted processings of the identical 16-sample experiment (4 donors $\times$ \{Untreated, Dex, Albuterol, Albuterol+Dex\}), separated by roughly a decade of annotation practice, and indexed in two different gene-identifier namespaces:
\begin{unlist}
\item \textbf{File 1}, the original 2013 submitter-supplied Cufflinks FPKM matrix, indexed by HGNC-style gene symbol, with explicit donor/treatment sample labels; and
\item \textbf{File 2}, a GEO-generated re-processing against genome build GRCh38.p13 with current NCBI/RefSeq annotation, indexed by NCBI (Entrez) GeneID, with samples labeled only by GSM accession and no treatment metadata retained in the file.
\end{unlist}
No Bioconductor package was needed for the analysis below; \texttt{DESeq2} could not be used because only FPKM, not raw counts, was available in either file, so differential expression is computed with a dependency-free log$_2$(FPKM+1) plus Welch $t$-test procedure and BH-FDR, stated here as a limitation relative to DESeq2's shrinkage-based approach. Full code is in \texttt{analysis/gse52778-real/real\_analysis.R} in the linked repository, and every number below is its direct, unedited output.

\subsection{Namespace/annotation-vintage divergence at the source}\label{sec:real-universe}

File 1 annotates 23{,}273 gene identifiers; File 2 annotates 39{,}376 -- 69\% more, at face value, a decade later. But an ORA background list should not be "every annotated row": using an inappropriate background is the single most common ORA-misuse pattern in the field, found in roughly 95\% of published analyses screened by \citet{wijesooriya2022orabackground}, precisely because raw annotation counts like this one overstate the effective background. Restricting to genes with any detected expression (mean FPKM$>0$) narrows the gap to 51\%; at mean FPKM$>0.1$, to 21\%; at mean FPKM$>1$, to 3.7\% (Fig.~\ref{fig:universe}). We report all four thresholds rather than only the headline figure: the sharp drop is itself an auditable finding (a naive, all-annotation background choice can inflate an apparent namespace-driven background discrepancy roughly seventeen-fold relative to an expression-filtered one), and it is the expression-filtered numbers, not the raw 69\%, that describe what a competently run ORA background comparison would actually see.

A second, string-level, reference-free measurement on File 1 alone: 2{,}153 of its 23{,}273 gene symbols (9.2\%) are genuinely provisional by HGNC's own conventions -- \texttt{LOC}\emph{nnnnnn} placeholders for genes without an approved symbol (5.5\%) and \texttt{C}\emph{n}\texttt{orf}\emph{n} open-reading-frame placeholders assigned before a gene's function is characterized (3.8\%, e.g.\ \texttt{C10orf10}, later renamed \texttt{DEPP1}); both classes are frequently retired or renamed.\footnote{A first pass at this measurement also counted \texttt{LINC}\emph{nnnn}-prefixed and dash-number-suffixed symbols as non-standard, reaching 11.3\%; both are in fact current, stable HGNC nomenclature (long non-coding RNA genes, and the \texttt{KRTAP}/\texttt{ERV}/\texttt{MIR} gene families respectively -- 205 of 338 dash-number symbols, 61\%), so we corrected the figure to count only the genuinely provisional classes.} This is a real, source-level instance of exactly the identifier instability Section~\ref{sec:matrix}'s versioned-Ensembl resolver is designed to catch, requiring no external reference database to measure.

\subsection{Ground-truth differential expression (File 1)}\label{sec:real-de}

Using File 1's own unambiguous per-sample treatment labels (Dex vs Untreated, $n=4$ vs $4$), 19{,}434 of 23{,}273 genes had non-zero mean expression and were tested; 536 (2.8\%) were significant at FDR$<0.05$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:volcano}). Of the eight canonical, glucocorticoid-receptor-induced marker genes used throughout this case study (FKBP5, TSC22D3/GILZ, ZBTB16, KLF15, PER1, DDIT4, CRISPLD2, PDK4),\footnote{DDIT4 is the panel's weakest member here, with the smallest log-fold change of the eight; it is retained since it appears alongside the others in the glucocorticoid-response literature this panel draws on, and dropping it would not change Section~\ref{sec:real-calibration}'s calibration result.} all eight show the expected positive log-fold change, and three -- FKBP5 ($\log_2$FC$=3.70$, FDR$=0.019$), ZBTB16 ($3.92$, $0.037$), and PER1 ($2.60$, $0.003$) -- clear FDR$<0.05$ under this simple test (Table~\ref{tab:markerde}); the other five have large effect sizes but do not clear FDR$<0.05$ with only four donors and no cross-gene variance shrinkage. This gap between large effect sizes and marginal significance is a genuine, secondary finding, but we can only partly attribute it: the test used here is both unpaired \emph{and} a per-gene $t$-test on FPKM, whereas DESeq2's standard analysis of this design is both paired (by donor) \emph{and} count-based with cross-gene variance shrinkage. GSE52778 pairs each of four donors across all four treatments, which an unpaired test cannot exploit; File 1's own headers identify each sample only by treatment and an anonymized specimen code (e.g.\ \texttt{Dex\_LL14}), sixteen distinct codes with neither a donor identifier nor a GSM accession linking a sample to the same donor's other three treatments. This is a distinct problem from the GSM-to-donor/treatment identification discussed in Section~\ref{sec:limitations} for File 2, whose different per-sample identifier (GSM accession) does not by itself resolve File 1's specimen-code-to-donor mapping; we report the unpaired result rather than construct a donor pairing by assumption. The five markers that miss FDR$<0.05$ here should therefore be read as a lower bound on how many a properly paired, shrinkage-based analysis would recover, not as evidence that method family alone explains the gap.

\subsection{A real calibration failure, reported rather than hidden}\label{sec:real-calibration}

The natural next step -- comparing File 1's and File 2's differential-expression calls to measure real cross-namespace fragility -- requires knowing which of File 2's 16 GSM-labeled columns received dexamethasone. That metadata is not present in the supplementary FPKM matrix as supplied (Section~\ref{sec:limitations} discusses the series-level metadata separately). Using GR-target biology as an orthogonal ground truth, we attempted to recover the labels directly from expression: an 8-gene composite $z$-score built from the canonical marker panel, clustered with $k$-means ($k=2$), was first calibrated against File 1's \emph{known} labels before being applied to File 2. Only 12 of 16 File-1 samples (75\%) are correctly recovered this way (Fig.~\ref{fig:calibration}, left) -- two Albuterol+Dex samples score as low as the Untreated/Albuterol arm, and two Dex/Untreated samples land on the wrong side of the split, reflecting real inter-donor variability that a small marker panel cannot fully average out. Applying the identical, identically-calibrated procedure to File 2 (Fig.~\ref{fig:calibration}, right) produces a superficially clean 8-vs-8 split, but the calibration accuracy means that split cannot be trusted for individual samples at better than roughly 3-in-4 odds. We report the recovered File-2 grouping in the repository for transparency but do not use it for any further differential-expression or fragility comparison, since doing so would conflate genuine namespace/annotation effects with label-recovery error.

This finding is directly relevant to the paper's broader argument: reproducing a published dataset's differential-expression result from its re-processed public form can be a metadata-availability problem in addition to an identifier-mapping problem, and a literature-validated marker panel is not automatically sufficient to substitute for missing sample metadata at the individual-sample level.

\section{Discussion and limitations}\label{sec:limitations}

Pathway-level fragility scores (GO/KEGG/Reactome/WikiPathways) on real data are not yet reported end to end: as of this commit the \texttt{targets} pipeline has not completed a successful continuous-integration run, and no fragility table exists yet under \texttt{analysis/airway-prototype/results/} -- both directly checkable in the linked repository. The obstacle is not annotation-data availability: the pipeline's continuous-integration job installs the full dependency stack (\texttt{DESeq2}, \texttt{org.Hs.eg.db}, \texttt{clusterProfiler}, \texttt{ReactomePA}, \texttt{biomaRt}, \texttt{fgsea}, \texttt{airway}) without error, and the differential-expression stage reliably completes against real data. It is instead a sequence of issues surfaced only by executing the real pipeline, each found from an actual run rather than by inspection and each fixed and regression-tested (Section~\ref{sec:validation}) as it was found. First, \texttt{clusterProfiler::enrichGO()} raises a hard error rather than an empty result on near-total mapping failure -- precisely the behavior the versioned-Ensembl positive control (Section~\ref{sec:matrix}) is designed to produce -- which \texttt{.run\_one\_enrichment()} now recognizes and recovers from. Second, \texttt{clusterProfiler::enrichWP()} requires an explicit \texttt{organism} argument that neither \texttt{enrichKEGG()} nor \texttt{enrichPathway()} require, which the wrapper omitted; it now defaults to \texttt{"Homo sapiens"}. Third, two of the five primary resolvers (versioned Ensembl, HGNC symbol via \texttt{biomaRt}) query Ensembl's live BioMart service, which briefly became unreachable across every mirror \texttt{biomaRt} itself tries during two consecutive runs and halted the entire ID-mapping step -- including for the three resolvers with no such dependency; \texttt{run\_mapping\_matrix()} now contains this specific failure to the branch it affects. Fourth, only \texttt{enrichGO()} accepts an arbitrary key type -- \texttt{enrichKEGG()}, \texttt{enrichPathway()}, and \texttt{enrichWP()} take only Entrez-family identifiers -- so KEGG, Reactome, and WikiPathways are computable only across the two Entrez-space branches (\texttt{entrez\_orgdb\_\_first}, \texttt{entrez\_orgdb\_\_list}), at which $F_p\in\{0,0.5,1\}$: too coarse for the entropy index or the $\tau_{lo},\tau_{hi}$ classification of Section~\ref{sec:fragility} to carry its intended resolution. GO Biological Process, computable across all six branches, is accordingly the primary namespace comparison this design supports; KEGG/Reactome/WikiPathways contribute only an Entrez-policy sub-comparison. Whether the enrichment and stability-metric stages downstream now run to completion is unconfirmed as of this commit. Confirming that, then extending to two further datasets (GSE159094, PBMC; GSE80651, podocyte) to test whether fragility reflects a gene set's definition or one dataset's noise, and anchoring against ChIP-seq-derived GR/NR3C1 target sets, is the immediate next step.

The empirical case study in Section~\ref{sec:real} uses two independently processed public forms of GSE52778 supplied directly by the corresponding author, and demonstrates both halves of this project's concern: a background-choice-sensitive gene-universe difference and real symbol-naming anomalies at the namespace/annotation level (Section~\ref{sec:real-universe}), and a related but distinct metadata-availability limitation in Section~\ref{sec:real-calibration}. The supplementary FPKM matrix analyzed there does not carry per-sample treatment labels, but GSE52778's GEO sample titles and characteristics do identify each sample by donor and treatment (e.g.\ GSM1275862 = donor N61311, untreated; GSM1275863 = N61311, dexamethasone) -- a distinction worth stating precisely, since the two claims have different implications for what can be recovered and how. We cross-checked this identification against File 1's already-known labels using the same GR-target composite score from Section~\ref{sec:real-calibration}: every sample identity checked this way is consistent with the composite score's expected direction (untreated/albuterol samples score low, dexamethasone/albuterol+dexamethasone samples score high), consistent with correct labeling. We report this consistency check rather than a full donor-by-treatment reassignment because confirmed labels cover only a subset of File 2's 16 samples; extending a partial, consistent pattern to the remaining samples by assumption would reintroduce the same unverified-labeling risk that Section~\ref{sec:real-calibration}'s calibration exercise was designed to avoid. A paired re-analysis of File 1 by donor, and the direct File-1-versus-File-2 cross-namespace differential-expression comparison this case study aims at, are immediate next steps once the complete sample metadata is in hand.

A further limitation is that the GSEA mode of the enrichment battery (Section~\ref{sec:design}) is interface-complete but not yet wired to a concrete pathway gene-set source (MSigDB Hallmark, C2:CP:KEGG, Reactome); only ORA is currently runnable end to end.

Finally, even once run on real data, three sources of instability must be kept analytically separate, as argued in Section~\ref{sec:fragility}: mapping-driven fragility, threshold-proximity flips that have nothing to do with identifier choice, and dataset-specific noise versus a pathway-intrinsic property. The framework reports the machinery to make each separation (threshold-sensitivity reporting, the mapping-noise null, and cross-dataset agreement), but none of that machinery substitutes for actually running it against real, independent datasets, which is future work.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

Gene-identifier-mapping choice is a routinely invisible step between differential expression and pathway enrichment. \texttt{idmapaudit} turns that choice into a measured, reusable quantity -- the per-pathway fragility score -- built on a stability-metric core that is fully specified, unit-tested against hand-computed answers, and released independently of the heavier Bioconductor dependencies its empirical use requires. Applied to two independent public processings of GSE52778, the same auditing approach identifies a background-choice-sensitive gene-universe difference, a quantified rate of genuinely provisional gene symbols in a decade-old annotation, and a marker-based sample-identity recovery procedure that caps at 75\% accuracy even under calibration -- a metadata-availability limitation adjacent to, and compounding, the identifier-mapping problem this tool audits. The pipeline that will produce full pathway-level fragility scores installs its complete annotation-database dependency stack on standard continuous-integration infrastructure without error, but has not yet completed a successful end-to-end run there (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}); reaching that pathway-level result across GO, KEGG, Reactome, and WikiPathways, and extending the case study to the two additional planned datasets, is the immediate next step.
